# AER case audit: monetary transmission, dividend signalling and cigarette addiction

**Status:** bounded documentary audit, 24 October 2026, by `decision_finance_sources`; not a replication or a complete re-coding of the AER surveys. The slide gate remains **closed**. These application papers are supporting evidence, not additions to the 35-work core corpus.

**Project judgement.** The retrieved originals support several of Hoover and Siegler's corrections, particularly the existence and interpretation of economic magnitudes in Becker, Grossman and Murphy. They also show why a blanket acquittal would go too far. Bernanke and Blinder explicitly rank predictive performance using significance tests before examining a second metric. Becker and colleagues use tests in model assessment, yet sometimes decline to let rejection decide their preferred specification. These are different propositions from whether either paper's economic conclusions are correct. The user subsequently supplied the final Bernheim–Wantz article: its contested passages now directly corroborate specific contextual corrections, while its identification and stability arguments remain subject to audit. See the [final follow-up](bernheim-wantz-final-followup.md).

## Bernanke and Blinder: predictive ranking is present, but it is not their only criterion

**Source claim — Ziliak and McCloskey.** The EJW version, p. 342, identifies the tables on original pp. 905 and 909 as examples of hierarchy by test statistics. The 2008 reply, p. 47, complains that the use of Granger-causality tests does not supply the sizes or substantive importance of variables and challenges an unconsidered significance threshold.

**Source claim — Hoover and Siegler.** *Sound and fury*, p. 10, notes that the tables do not literally rank their entries or display significance asterisks. It defends the tests as evidence concerning incremental predictive information and points to Tables 2 and 4 as assessments of economic relevance through explained variance.

**Source observation — original article.** Tables 1 and 3 are organised by variable, rather than sorted by the test results, and do not use asterisk tiers. However, the accompanying prose on pp. 905–906 explicitly uses low marginal significance levels to compare predictive performance, labels the funds rate the best variable by the Granger-causality criterion, and treats the conventional 5% level as an absolute standard. The absence of literal stars therefore does not establish that statistical rankings play no role.

The same discussion supplies a substantial qualification. On p. 906, the authors describe a case in which an exogenous policy variable could fail a conditional Granger test because another included variable transmits its effect. They then compare variance decompositions, acknowledging dependence on ordering and horizon and explaining that the two measures convey different information. Pages 907–910 interpret the variance shares and explicitly discuss disagreement between the two criteria. These are substantive comparisons, not merely a list of p-values.

**Visually checked example.** On p. 909, Table 3 reports a marginal significance level of **0.86** for FUNDS in the industrial-production equation. Table 4 on that same page nevertheless reports a FUNDS variance share of **6.6%** in panel A and **21.8%** when the ordering changes in panel B. The numerical contrast illustrates their argument about conditional predictability and ordering. It is not an independent estimate of policy effectiveness, and it does not imply that either variance share is uniquely correct.

**Project interpretation.** The narrow claim that these tables literally order coefficients by test statistics is inaccurate. The broader observation that test statistics are used to rank predictive performance is supported by the prose. Conversely, the claim that the study never considers a substantive measure is contradicted by its variance-decomposition discussion. Those variance shares provide a quantitative account of predictive contribution, but they are not by themselves a welfare measure or the structural effect of a policy intervention.

Identification matters separately: pp. 901–902 warn against structural interpretation of unrestricted VARs and state alternative identifying assumptions. Page 910 explicitly declines to claim a conclusive separation of reserve-demand and reserve-supply forces. Thus neither small p-values nor large variance shares can, by themselves, establish the monetary-policy interpretation. The article's conditional argument and its qualifications should be preserved in teaching.

**Open questions.** How robust are forecast performance and policy interpretations to estimation uncertainty, alternative identification schemes, specification search and evaluation outside the estimation sample? The present documentary check does not answer these questions or verify the claimed empirical robustness.

## Bernheim and Wantz: final text, magnitude interpretation and regime tests

**Source claim — Ziliak and McCloskey.** EJW p. 340 quotes discussion of negative, statistically non-significant high-rating interaction coefficients as an example of focusing on sign without magnitude. Page 342 identifies the original p. 547 table as another test-statistic hierarchy. The 2008 reply, pp. 47–48, challenges the presumption that a conventional regime-change test is automatically an appropriate scientific decision rule.

**Source claim — Hoover and Siegler.** *Sound and fury*, p. 11, says the p. 547 table's only star denotes an alternative scenario, not a significance tier. It also says the quotation from p. 543 omits an immediately following distinction between economic and statistical effects and a subsequent discussion of bang-for-buck magnitudes.

**Source observation — December 1992 draft only.** In the NBER draft, pp. 23–25, the authors distinguish the economic and statistical aspects of the interaction estimates and qualify the bond-rating evidence. They then calculate the change in abnormal return associated with a dividend change and compare that with observed announcement magnitudes. This is an interpretation of economic units, not merely coefficient signs. The corresponding Table 4, printed p. 35, is a structural-change table organised as tests 1, 1*, 2 and 3. Its star distinguishes the alternative first comparison described on p. 28; it does not denote a p-value category.

Pages 27–29 explain the regime-comparison rationale. The draft recognises that unequal time spans and sample sizes can change rejection behaviour, then constructs an additional comparison intended to address that concern. That is relevant evidence against portraying the design as wholly oblivious to how tests depend on sample size. It is not a demonstration that the resulting comparison identifies a tax mechanism or controls every relevant error.

**Source observation — final article.** The [final follow-up](bernheim-wantz-final-followup.md) now directly checks pp. 543–549. Page 543 immediately distinguishes economic from statistical effects after the passage quoted by the critics. Page 544 interprets a coefficient as a 3.88-percentage-point return response to a one-percentage-point change in the dividend/share-price ratio, then compares an illustrative 0.54-percentage-point response with the sample's 1.19% average abnormal return. These are substantive interpretations with reported uncertainty, not merely signs. The one asterisk in Table 4, p. 547, labels test 1*, the alternative comparison of regimes Ib and IIa explained on p. 549; it is not a significance tier.

**Project interpretation.** Direct final reading therefore verifies the narrow Hoover–Siegler corrections. It does not validate the model or acquit every inferential step. The final study conducts direct comparisons: n. 17, p. 547, reports tests of differences in bang-for-buck estimates, and pp. 548–549 explain joint tests of 12 coefficient pairs. Portraying these as only comparisons of separate within-regime significance would itself misdescribe the paper.

The narrower concern is the move from a non-rejected equality test within regime II to strong stability language, and from the pattern of tests to discrimination between causal accounts. Test 1* explicitly addresses unequal durations and precision; it does not establish that every plausible alternative has been eliminated. An economically justified equivalence region, joint uncertainty and an identification analysis remain distinct needs. Magnitude interpretation and an intelligently designed test sequence are evidence of substantive reasoning, not proof of the proposed mechanism.

**Open questions.** Audit the regime and bond-rating comparisons, their error properties and alternative explanations; reconcile the table/sample description noted in the final follow-up; and inspect the complete draft/final changes if making a claim about revision history. No revised survey score or replication follows from this reading.

## Becker, Grossman and Murphy: magnitude interpretation and non-mechanical model assessment

**Source claim — Ziliak and McCloskey.** EJW pp. 348–349 argues that tests and signs govern the inference of addiction, criticises the interpretation of significant smuggling and consumption coefficients, acknowledges reported elasticities but says they are never interpreted, and challenges the estimation and use of aggregate data. Its narrative dates the article June 1999, although its reference list and the original identify June **1994**. The 2008 reply, p. 48, explicitly concedes that magnitudes are discussed and instead argues that the preceding selection of which magnitudes to examine is Fisherian and produces biased or inconsistent coefficients.

**Source claim — Hoover and Siegler.** *Sound and fury*, pp. 11–13, points to the introductory price responses, elasticity discussion and economically implausible discount rates, arguing that the article does interpret magnitudes. Note 9, p. 32, additionally points to a simulation despite the negative questionnaire entry for simulation. Their defence is not a replication of the demand model or a demonstration of its correctness.

**Source observation — original article.** Pages 396–397 interpret the estimated intertemporal effects and explain implications for tax revenue. On p. 407, Table 4 and its accompanying prose distinguish permanent from temporary price changes, anticipated from unanticipated changes, and short-run from long-run responses. For example, model (i)'s long-run price elasticity is **−0.734**, compared with a short-run elasticity of **−0.407**. The authors also assess the economic plausibility of implied discount factors. These facts directly contradict a literal claim that they never interpret the elasticities.

Tests nonetheless matter. Pages 403–404 describe beginning with a myopic model, examining whether future prices predict current consumption, and using a Wu test when discussing OLS versus instrumental-variable estimation. Page 404 uses the significance of the smuggling variables as evidence of their importance. That language merits scrutiny: a small p-value alone does not measure the amount of smuggling or its consequences. The authors also say the future-price tests do not provide definitive evidence for their structural rational-addiction model, because the tests do not impose its full mechanism.

The stronger answer to the 2008 selection criticism lies on pp. 408–410. A Hausman test rejects the legitimacy of future instruments under stated maintained assumptions. The authors explicitly caution against placing too much weight on that rejection, discuss weak predictors and misspecification, re-estimate with alternative lag restrictions, and perform a covariance-based sensitivity simulation. They explain that preference between the specifications depends on beliefs about forecast errors and structural misspecification. This published account combines tests, economic reasoning and sensitivity analysis. It does not support describing every specification choice as mechanically determined by a threshold.

The simulation begins at the bottom of p. 409 and is developed on p. 410, including Table 6. It varies an assumed relationship between expected and actual future quantities and examines implications for consumption coefficients and the elasticity ratio. Its existence supports Hoover and Siegler's description of an actual simulation. It does **not**, by itself, establish that the negative Question 17 code is wrong: the original 1996 discussion on p. 104 also says that statistical significance should not screen the inputs to a simulation. The root agent's separate [book reading ledger](cult-2008-reading.md), pp. 82–83, records the explicit negative-coding rule when statistical significance alone selects the coefficients later simulated. A coding audit must therefore examine the input-selection rule as well as the simulation's presence. The non-mechanical specification assessment on pp. 408–410 is pertinent counterevidence to a broad threshold-only account, but this selected reading is not a complete reconstruction of every input decision and does not replace the survey score.

**Project qualifications.** Showing interpretation and sensitivity analysis does not validate the instruments, prove forward-looking individual behaviour from state aggregates, or quantify the strength of addiction without model assumptions. The inference of the discount factor is explicitly unreliable in the authors' assessment on pp. 411–412. Conversely, simply asserting that the aggregate analysis is an ecological fallacy does not identify which model implications fail: that requires examining aggregation assumptions and the estimand.

There is also a specific caution against a blanket defence. In footnote 12, spanning pp. 411–412, the original equates a non-significant restriction with a valid imposed discount factor. Taken as proof of the restriction, that interpretation is too strong: failure to reject does not establish its truth. This local concern is distinct from the empirically stronger correction that magnitudes are interpreted and tests are not the sole determinant of their preferred specification.

**Transcription check.** The original p. 407 gives the upper implied interest rate as **222.6%**; the quotation in *Sound and fury*, p. 12, gives **226.6%**. The original page was inspected visually. This small quotation discrepancy does not change the economic-plausibility argument, but should not be propagated.

**Open questions.** Revisit aggregation, the exclusion restrictions, forecast-error assumptions, weak-instrument behaviour, covariance estimation and alternative addiction models. No results were re-estimated and no general verdict on rational addiction follows from this documentary audit.

## What the case comparisons do and do not establish

**Project interpretation.** Three distinct checks must remain separate: whether the quoted account is faithful, whether the empirical procedure is justified, and whether the economic conclusion is true. The original texts can correct misdescription without validating the estimates. They can also reveal local uses of significance that merit criticism without demonstrating that every step in the analysis is defective.

The selected cases are not a random audit of the AER universe. They do not identify the prevalence of misuse or settle the questionnaire's construct validity. The 2008 reply's distinction between initial selection and later interpretation must be taken seriously, but it requires evidence about the actual selection process. For Becker et al., the reported treatment of adverse test results and specification sensitivity is important counterevidence to its broad characterisation.
