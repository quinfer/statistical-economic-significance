## The disputed passages in context

### 1. Signs, magnitude and the scope of the quotation

**Source observations — final article, pp. 539 and 543–544.** The authors distinguish their question about how the return response varies with observable costs from estimating its level alone (p. 539). On p. 543 they report negative high-rating interaction coefficients, qualify conventional non-significance specifically for **Table 1**, then immediately distinguish larger economic and statistical effects in Table 2. The same paragraph discusses Table 2's precision and uses the negative rating–response relation, together with its cost interpretation, as evidence favouring signalling. Thus the context contains both economic language and an inferential argument.

**Source comparison — Ziliak–McCloskey and Hoover–Siegler.** The final JSE article, pp. **534–535**, was visually read for this follow-up, in addition to the earlier EJW pp. 340–342 reading. Its p. 534 example interpolates four regressions into the quoted passage but omits the original's Table 1 qualifier before the non-significance statement. Its p. 535 includes the p. 547 table among examples of a hierarchy of test statistics. Hoover and Siegler, *Sound and fury*, p. 11, object to this selection of context and point to the following economic/statistical sentence and subsequent magnitude discussion. The final text confirms those omitted features. Their description of a passing remark without an inference is defensible only for the isolated Table 1 qualification; it should not be extended to the complete paragraph, which explicitly draws a theory-directed inference from the rating relationship.

**Source observations — final p. 544, Tables 1–2 and following paragraph.** The authors report representative bang-for-buck estimates and standard errors, then interpret a Table 1 estimate in return units and against observed dividend changes. Their example translates a one-percentage-point increase in dividends relative to share price into **3.88 percentage points** of abnormal return. An additional average-sized dividend increase—approximately **0.14% of share price**—corresponds to about **0.54 percentage points**, compared with a sample average abnormal return of **1.19%**. These are the authors' fitted quantities and benchmark, not independently replicated economic effects. Their assumptions and conditional specification still matter.

**Project interpretation.** This is direct evidence of magnitude interpretation, not just numbers left in a table. It weakens the quoted example as evidence that the paper disregards size. The theoretical interest in a direction can also be intelligible without a policy threshold for how large an effect should be. Neither point establishes that the inference is correct, that any size would have practical value, or that every questionnaire item should receive a positive code.

### 2. Table 4, direct contrasts and non-rejection

**Source observations — final pp. 547–549.** Table 4 has two panels. Panel A presents representative bang-for-buck estimates with standard errors for tax regimes and subregimes; panel B reports joint tests. The rows are not sorted by statistic or p-value. The asterisk in **test 1*** identifies an alternative regime comparison, explained on p. 549 as addressing different period lengths and precision. It does not encode a significance tier.

Footnote **17 on p. 547 explicitly reports tests of differences in estimated bang-for-buck between regimes**. The article therefore must not be characterised as substituting a difference between separate significance labels for a test of the difference. It also recognises that the representative magnitude comparison is incomplete: bang-for-buck depends on covariates, and changing representative conditions can change the pattern (pp. 547–548). It proceeds to joint tests of the corresponding regression coefficients and uses those tests as context for the magnitude panel.

On p. 549 the authors treat rejection for between-regime comparisons and non-rejection for the within-regime comparison as evidence of a change pattern aligned with tax reform. They address differing sample durations and one corporate-tax alternative, and use the alleged within-regime stability against certain competing explanations. The conclusion, pp. 549–550, presents the findings as favourable to signalling.

**Project interpretation.** The table is not well described as an ordering of importance by stars, and the final article makes an explicit effort to connect magnitudes, contrasts and mechanisms. The narrower unresolved issue is the evidential force of the **within-regime non-rejection**. No pre-specified equivalence margin or equivalence test is provided in these passages; inability to reject equality does not alone establish that change was economically negligible. Evaluating the joint-test pattern also requires its uncertainty model, power against relevant alternatives, and identifying assumptions. This audit has not recomputed those properties or established whether the proposed alternatives exhaust plausible explanations.

### 3. Specification choice and the later reply

**Source observations — final pp. 541–543, especially footnotes 12, 14 and 15.** The article explains variable construction, dividend-increase restrictions, tax-transition exclusions and earnings-announcement conflicts. It compares a specification whose dividend interactions are restricted to the theory variables with a more inclusive one. Footnote 15 says the focal tax interaction also appears in parsimonious regressions, but the authors report inclusive regressions because other economic factors may affect its magnitude, including inflation in the later period. Additional specifications and samples are discussed as unreported checks; their asserted robustness has not been independently checked.

**Source claim — McCloskey–Ziliak's 2008 reply, pp. 47–48.** The reply challenges automatic endorsement of a conventional regime test and distinguishes discussing some magnitudes from choosing variables or deciding importance through significance alone. It concentrates its subsequent selection accusation on the addiction study; it is not a new passage-level demonstration that Bernheim–Wantz used a purely significance-based selection algorithm.

**Project interpretation.** The final specification discussion supplies substantive reasons beyond a simple significance filter, so a blanket allegation needs a specified contrary step in the paper. But the existence of these reasons does not prove that every reporting or selection decision was appropriate. The general distinction between magnitude reporting and selection validity remains important; this reading resolves particular allegations and quotation context, not the full research pipeline.

## Version and internal-document cautions

The final version is substantively different from the draft in the checked material. Its representative estimate in the magnitude example is **3.88**, whereas the draft example is **3.28**. Final Table 4 adds the representative-estimate **panel A**; the draft's Table 4, p. 35, is a structural-test table. The final empirical specifications include capacity utilisation as an additional theory-linked variable. Draft values and table descriptions must therefore not silently stand in for the final source.

There is an apparent internal sample-label inconsistency in the final article. **Table 2's heading on p. 544 says 1962–1988**, whereas **p. 543** expressly identifies its earnings-conflict-excluded sample as **1978–1988**, explaining that the earnings data begin in 1978. Both were visually checked. Record the discrepancy; this audit does not determine the underlying estimation sample from the data or silently repair the heading.

The [book's conditional simulation criterion](cult-2008-reading.md) and the [coding instrument](aer-coding-audit.md) prevent a mechanical inference from the presence of magnitude calculations to all favourable codes. No new article score, corrected prevalence of misuse, test of the tax mechanism, or complete draft/final identity follows from this case.
