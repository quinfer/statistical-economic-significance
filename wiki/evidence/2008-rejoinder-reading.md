## Project assessment

**Uncertainty versus rejection.** Page 58's warning is strongest when an estimate is treated as a measurement of an unknown population or causal effect. Under a normal likelihood, an estimate and its standard error jointly determine how well different magnitudes fit the data. A large raw estimate cannot establish a large underlying effect by itself. But this does not imply that a zero-null rejection is necessary before action, or before evidence for negligibility can be useful. The [analytical checks](mathematical-checks.md) supply counterexamples.

**A technical error in an otherwise useful defence.** Page 58's parenthetical p-value definition conflates a realised tail probability with Type I error probability. Under a specified null and sampling model, the p-value is a probability of a test statistic at least as extreme as observed. A test's size concerns its repeated-sampling rejection probability under a null; neither is the posterior probability that this rejection is wrong. This is a local criticism, supported by the [ASA statement](../papers/wasserstein-lazar-2016.md), principles 1–2. It does not invalidate all of the rejoinder's uncertainty arguments.

**Intervals and tests.** Pages 60–61 correctly motivate attention to an observation model, but mathematical duality does not eliminate differences in communication and the question being tested. An interval compared with an economically justified nonzero margin can answer a different practical question from a binary zero-null result. Conversely, an interval without scale, identification or an importance criterion cannot deliver economic relevance. The reply's concern about substantive judgement therefore survives a qualified duality argument.

**Counterfactuals and dependence.** The sampling framework cannot be dismissed solely because only one time-series path is observed. Equally, postulating a superpopulation does not establish that a particular stochastic model, stationarity assumption or standard error is adequate. A finite-population descriptive accounting claim need not share the inferential requirements of a causal counterfactual. Page 60 should not be read as a proof that every estimate is useless without hypothetical repetition.

**Public evidence and diverse losses.** The question on p. 59 prevents a facile answer that every scientific report must optimise a single decision-maker's utility. Scientific reporting can present magnitudes, uncertainty and assumptions for multiple users. It does not follow that local policy or investment choices should avoid explicit losses. Those are different audiences and tasks.

**Survey claims remain open.** Pages 65–66 motivate a reproducible audit of construct validity, item weights and dependence, coding reliability, and article-to-score mapping. They do not themselves supply our replication. An audit must preserve disagreements over whether the coding captures omission of substantive discussion, mistaken inferential reasoning, or something else. Omitted articles and keyword-search denominators cannot settle all these questions at once.

**Scope correction.** Page 65 blocks presenting this exchange as a close review of the full 2008 book. At that exchange-only milestone, the [book note](../papers/ziliak-mccloskey-2008-cult.md) was at publisher-description reading depth; the later evidence section below records its upgrade to selected direct reading. No historical or welfare-harm verdict follows from reading three polemical articles.
