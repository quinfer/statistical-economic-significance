## Verified corrections and qualifications

**Source claim — procedural posture:** the Supreme Court affirmed the Ninth Circuit because the complaint adequately alleged materiality and scienter. It assumed the complaint's allegations true at this stage (pp. 32, 45–47). Section II B ends by expressly distinguishing adequate pleading from ultimately proving the allegations (p. 50). This was not a trial finding that Zicam caused anosmia or a final finding of securities-fraud liability.

**Source claim — materiality and causation:** § II A rejects statistical significance as an indispensable condition for materiality. It discusses non-significant evidence used by medical researchers and regulators (pp. 40–43), but does not define a general standard of reliable causal evidence (p. 41). Statistical significance remains potentially relevant (p. 43). Reports of adverse events alone are insufficient; their source, content and context matter (p. 44). Nor does materiality by itself impose a universal disclosure duty under the provisions considered (pp. 44–45).

**Source claim — date:** lecture page 23 visibly dates *Basic v. Levinson* to 1976. The correct date is **1988**. *Basic*, pp. 231–232, adopts the materiality standard from **TSC Industries, Inc. v. Northway, Inc.**, 426 U.S. 438 (1976). Confusion between those cases is a plausible explanation, not a verified account of how the lecture error arose.

## Project interpretation

The case is a strong illustration of why an information-dependent decision cannot be reduced to a test of a sharp causal null. A report can change an investor's assessment of a product's commercial prospects even while the strength or mechanism of a medical causal relationship remains uncertain. The opinion's application emphasises alleged risks to the firm's leading product alongside its public statements (p. 47). This is compatible with weighing uncertain consequences; it is not a numerical expected-utility calculation or an econometric estimate of an economically important causal effect.

Two opposite readings would overstate the holding. Treating it as an endorsement of inference-free claims ignores the Court's demand for contextual evidence and its explicit caution that occurrence after use does not establish causation. Treating it as irrelevant to causation ignores the actual discussion in pp. 40–46. The narrower point is that causal evidence, investor materiality, a duty to disclose and the procedural sufficiency of a complaint are distinct questions.

The lecture itself recognises a boundary: page 26 announces what the Supreme Court **did not** say before advancing Ziliak's stronger critique. Preserve that distinction rather than attributing the lecture's entire methodological position to the Court. Page 25's request for additional contextual information is consistent with the opinion, but oral-argument questions on pages 22–24 are not holdings.
