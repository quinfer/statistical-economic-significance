## What the audit can establish

**Project analysis.** The strongest contribution is a direct examination of whether published claims of negligible relationships are accompanied by sufficiently informative estimates under explicit benchmarks. This addresses the distinction in [C003](../claims/C003-equivalence.md): failure to reject zero and evidence supporting a practically small effect are different achievements. The proposed remedy remains statistical inference, with substantive judgement entering through the margin. It therefore helps sharpen the [2008 exchange](2008-exchange.md) without resolving its disagreements merely by replacing one test with another.

The observed equivalence-testing failure rate, ETFR, must remain separate from the frequency of false substantive claims. A conventional Type II error probability conditions on a specified non-null effect and a testing procedure. An observed collection of equivalence failures does not reveal those true effects. For example, imagine that every underlying effect is zero but the estimates have large standard errors. Many tests can fail to establish equivalence, while their conventional non-rejections of zero are correct. Thus high ETFR can reveal inadequate evidential support for bounded negligibility without proving that many underlying relationships are economically large. Conversely, an equivalence failure can sometimes accompany affirmative evidence of a meaningful effect; failure alone does not classify the situation.

Scope matters as well. Accessibility and conformability restrictions can select a distinctive subset of research. Before extending conclusions across economics, compare included and excluded studies, clarify claim-selection rules, and examine dependence among estimates and claims within articles. Giving each claim or article equal weight answers different descriptive questions. Surveyed researchers’ acceptable failure rates provide normative judgements about the chosen task; they do not supply missing observations of true effects or establish a representative professional consensus.

## Meaningful margins need two kinds of justification

**Project analysis.** Standardised units permit broad comparisons, but a common numerical margin does not create a common economic meaning. The same standard-deviation change can have different consequences across outcomes, populations, exposure changes and policy settings. A benchmark audit is consequently best read alongside sensitivity analyses and examples justified in natural units. The margin should be defended for the estimand and use, rather than inferred from its convenience or prevalence in a methodological convention.

Two different problems arise when a margin is too wide. With a fixed, genuinely prespecified margin and a valid test, widening the margin changes the null hypothesis. It can make an economically unhelpful equivalence claim easier to establish without necessarily violating nominal Type I error control for that new null. By contrast, selecting or expanding margins after examining the focal results can alter the procedure’s error properties. A statement that wide margins inflate error must identify which null, which substantive standard and which selection procedure define the error. Preregistration can document a poor substantive question just as faithfully as a useful one.

## Equivalence and economic viability are different targets

**Project mathematical analysis; illustrative assumptions.** Suppose an intervention changes an outcome by an unknown causal effect \(\delta\), each outcome unit is worth \(V_Y>0\), and the intervention costs \(V_D>0\). Net benefit is

\[
NB(\delta)=V_Y\delta-V_D.
\]

The proposition that benefits fail to cover costs is one-sided: \(\delta<V_D/V_Y\). Establishing symmetric equivalence within \((-V_D/V_Y,V_D/V_Y)\) is sufficient for that proposition, but unnecessary. A strongly negative effect fails symmetric equivalence while plainly failing the cost-coverage criterion. Choose the test according to the intended claim; use two-sided bounds when both directions have substantive relevance. See [equivalence and meaningful margins](../concepts/equivalence.md).

Even a well-chosen one-sided test is not a complete decision rule. Uncertain valuations, heterogeneous benefits, risk preferences, feasible alternatives and the value of waiting can matter. The assumed causal interpretation must also be defended: assigning monetary values to an association does not identify an intervention’s effect.

## Independence requires a protocol

**Project analysis.** Consulting different people does not by itself make margin selection independent of the focal results. A researcher who has seen those results can influence questions, respondent recruitment, response exclusions, aggregation and the number of surveys attempted. A credible retrospective protocol should blind respondents to focal estimates where feasible, fix elicitation and aggregation choices before comparing answers with results, report all responses and prevent selective repeated elicitation. The resulting median or other summary remains an explicitly chosen normative aggregation rule.

These qualifications support a constructive next step: retain Fitzgerald as a directly relevant contemporary source, distinguish documented inferential insufficiency from claims about truth, and audit its proposed remedies using the same care applied to conventional significance testing. This source review does not clear the knowledge-base, synthesis or slide review gate.
