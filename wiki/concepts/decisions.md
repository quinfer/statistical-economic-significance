# Decisions under uncertainty

**Illustration.** A committee must decide whether to adopt a programme for an eligible population. The estimated average benefit is £400 per person over one year, with a known standard error of £500. Assume the study identifies the effect and the estimate is unbiased and normally distributed. The two-sided p-value for zero benefit is about 0.42. What information would you ask for before recommending adoption?

Take a normal prior on average benefit with mean £0 and standard deviation £1,000. Combining it with the study gives a posterior mean of £320. Suppose the committee chooses between adoption at a known cost and keeping the status quo at zero payoff. If it maximises expected net benefit using linear utility, a cost of £100 per person leaves an expected gain of £220, favouring adoption. At £500 the expected gain becomes −£180, favouring the status quo. The test against zero uses the same estimate and standard error in both cases. The committee’s recommendation is conditional on the stated prior and payoff. ## Are you choosing an action or judging a rule?

**Project interpretation.** Choosing after seeing the data requires feasible actions, a description of uncertainty and a way to value consequences. The expected-utility approach in the example uses a posterior distribution and a utility function to compare adoption and the status quo. A different weighting of benefits and harms can change the choice. One action may even be preferable across all the states you regard as feasible. A recommendation under such a criterion answers a question about action. Establishing the magnitude of an unknown population effect requires its own evidence. [Action without rejection](../claims/C002-action-without-rejection.md) · [Economic significance and inference](../claims/C001-independence.md)

To evaluate a data-dependent rule before seeing the data, pick a possible state and imagine repeatedly observing data in that state. Expected regret is the difference between the best welfare you could achieve if you knew the state and the rule’s average welfare over those samples. A minimax-regret rule chooses the rule with the smallest worst expected regret across the states considered. State which actions and states are available, how samples arise and what counts as welfare. [Critical synthesis](../../synthesis/critical-synthesis.md)

**Open question.** If the committee could run a small, reversible pilot, what would it need to learn to justify the cost of waiting? Explain whose welfare you count, what could reverse your choice, and whether the recommendation survives a less favourable prior or higher costs. [Finance applications](finance.md)

## Evidence and further reading

- **Source claim.** Manski’s treatment-choice paper examines how choosing a treatment differs from testing a hypothesis. His 2021 paper broadens model-based evaluation of decisions. Kandel and Stambaugh offer an investment application, consulted at abstract level. Wald’s book is verified at the bibliographic level and awaits substantive reading. [Manski on treatment choice](../papers/manski-2019-treatment-choice.md) · [Manski on econometrics for decisions](../papers/manski-2021-econometrics-decisions.md) · [Kandel and Stambaugh](../papers/kandel-stambaugh-1996-asset-allocation.md) · [Wald](../papers/wald-1950-decision-functions.md)
- **Source claim.** In their partial-identification setting and specified prior class, Aradillas Fernández et al. distinguish minimax prior risk before seeing the data from worst posterior regret after seeing them. The criteria can recommend different rules, and randomisation can be optimal under both. The claims are bounded by the assumptions and the selected sections consulted. **Project interpretation.** Say when the rule is evaluated, which priors and states enter the calculation, and how loss is measured.
