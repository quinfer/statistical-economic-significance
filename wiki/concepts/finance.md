# Finance: economic value, discovery and replication

**Illustration.** A fixed hypothetical portfolio has an estimated monthly gross alpha of 20 basis points, with a standard error of 2 basis points. Alpha here is the intercept in a specified factor model. One basis point is 0.01 percentage points. Assume an unbiased normal estimate with known standard error, a valid factor model and an unchanged trading regime. With a known monthly cost of 10 basis points at the proposed scale, the estimated net alpha is 10. What happens if the monthly cost is 25?

Keeping the portfolio fixed and subtracting known, additive costs shifts the estimate and confidence interval by the same amount. The 95% interval for gross alpha is about 16.08 to 23.92 basis points per month. At the higher cost, estimated net alpha is −5 basis points and the interval is −8.92 to −1.08. Evidence of positive gross alpha can coexist with evidence of negative net alpha under these assumptions. Ask how the calculation changes when costs are estimated or rise with trading size. ## What would have to replicate?

**Project interpretation.** Start by saying what the original study estimated. A predictor’s regression coefficient measures an association in a specified model. A factor-model alpha describes a portfolio’s average unexplained return under its benchmark. An implementable payoff takes account of costs and trading constraints. An investor’s utility gain also depends on risk, portfolio exposures, horizon and uncertainty about the parameters. Capacity can change the answer when a strategy scales up. [Decisions under uncertainty](decisions.md)

**Open question.** Suppose a published predictor works in its original dataset and weakens in a later period. What would you look for to distinguish extensive searching from a shift in market conditions or a change in implementation? Compare the original and replication samples, portfolios, benchmarks and definitions of success before declaring a contradiction. ## Evidence and further reading

- **Source claim.** Harvey, Liu and Zhu examine extensive searching. McLean and Pontiff examine returns outside the original sample and after publication. Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen offer a more favourable assessment of replication under their design. Compare the targets and success criteria of these studies. [Harvey, Liu and Zhu](../papers/harvey-liu-zhu-2016-cross-section.md) · [McLean and Pontiff](../papers/mclean-pontiff-2016-predictability.md) · [Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen](../papers/jensen-kelly-pedersen-2023-replication.md)
- **Source claim.** Harvey and Liu address missed discoveries alongside false discoveries. The abstract of Kandel and Stambaugh examines portfolio choice under Bayesian parameter uncertainty. The papers inform different stages of evaluating an investment. [Harvey and Liu](../papers/harvey-liu-2020-false-missed.md) · [Kandel and Stambaugh](../papers/kandel-stambaugh-1996-asset-allocation.md)
- **Source claim.** The abstract, introduction and selected sections including II.D–III of Menkveld et al. examine how analysts’ choices matter on common data. **Project interpretation.** Compare their targets and analysis choices before treating dispersion as a measure of bias or a variance term in a specific model. [Menkveld et al.](../papers/menkveld-etal-2024-nonstandard-errors.md) · [Uncertainty beyond sampling](uncertainty-beyond-sampling.md)
- **Reading limit.** Mitton’s proposal on standardised effects is supported here at abstract level. The article appeared online in 2022 and in its journal issue in 2024. It remains a candidate for further reading. The evidence on this page informs how to evaluate strategies and stops short of verifying a tradable payoff or realised welfare.
