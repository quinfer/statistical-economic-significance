# Identification and transportability

**Illustration.** A lender finds that borrowers who voluntarily joined a programme had a lower one-year default rate than those who did not. There are enough observations to estimate the difference precisely. Would the volunteers have defaulted as rarely without the programme? Think of a borrower characteristic that might explain both enrolment and default.

## What can the study tell you about the target effect?

**Project interpretation.** The difference in default rates describes what happened to the two groups. The causal question compares what would happen to the same target group with and without the programme. If borrowers who already manage their finances carefully are more likely to enrol and less likely to default, self-selection can produce a gap even if the programme has zero effect. A larger sample gives a more precise estimate of the observed gap under the sampling model. You still need a design or substantive assumptions that connect the gap to the causal effect. [Economic significance](economic-significance.md) · [Precision and economic relevance](../claims/C009-identification.md)

Identification asks what the observed distribution can reveal about the target under those assumptions, even with arbitrarily precise data. When the distribution and assumptions rule out some values but leave several possibilities for the effect, the effect is *partially identified*. If the lender randomly assigns offers, the comparison by assignment can identify the effect of receiving an offer in the study population under suitable conditions. To learn the effect of participation when some borrowers decline the offer, specify what additional assumptions link assignment and participation.

**Open question.** A second lender with a different borrower mix wants to apply the result. Which borrower characteristics might change the programme’s effect, and how would you check whether the study covers the relevant groups? That is a question of *transportability*. Confounding, changing conditions over time and responses to policy at scale also merit further study. The current corpus leaves gaps in specialised economics sources on these methods. ## Evidence and further reading

- **Source claim.** Selected passages in Deaton and Cartwright examine what randomisation warrants and what applying findings elsewhere requires. Their challenge concerns the guarantees sometimes attributed to experiments. Manski motivates considering states outside a chosen model.
