# Statistical and economic significance

A lender estimates that a programme reduces the one-year default rate among eligible borrowers by 1.1 percentage points. The lender set one percentage point as the benchmark for a worthwhile reduction. The study’s 95% confidence interval runs from 0.71 to 1.49 percentage points. A colleague points out that it excludes zero and recommends adoption. Which part of that recommendation can you defend from the study?

This is a synthetic example. The estimate and the benchmark use the same units and refer to the same population and horizon. The interval still contains effects on both sides of the benchmark. To make a recommendation, you would also need to know what the programme costs, what avoiding defaults is worth and what the lender could do instead. Keep the example in mind as you read the chapter. It gives you a way to test each argument about what statistical evidence can establish. [Worked calculation and assumptions](../exercises/01-magnitude-evidence-action.md)

## 1. Which claim are you trying to establish?

Suppose you are writing the results section of the study. Before choosing a test, write the sentence you want a reader to be able to defend. A claim about the size of last year’s losses, a claim about the effect of the programme and a recommendation to expand the programme each give you a different job.

| Claim | What you need to justify |
|---|---|
| A reduction of one percentage point would matter. | The units, eligible population, horizon, costs, alternatives and whose gains or losses count. |
| Recorded losses last year exceeded a threshold. | The completeness and quality of the records and the relevance of the period and population described. |
| The programme causes a reduction larger than one percentage point. | A design and assumptions that identify the causal effect, an estimate with a defensible account of uncertainty, and evidence about where the effect lies relative to the benchmark. |
| The lender should adopt the programme. | Feasible actions, their consequences under uncertainty and a criterion for choosing among them. |

**Project interpretation.** The first claim can be justified before sampling. A lender might have a break-even calculation that motivates the benchmark, while a regulator might care about effects on particular groups of borrowers. The same average reduction can have different consequences at different scales and for different people. The choice of benchmark is part of the economic argument.

For a purely descriptive claim, a complete and accurately measured ledger may answer the question. An accounting identity can translate known inputs into a total without sampling extrapolation. Change the question to next year’s losses, or to losses that would have occurred without the programme, and the ledger only gives you part of the answer. Measurement, extrapolation and the missing counterfactual can all matter. [Economic significance and inference](../wiki/claims/C001-independence.md) · [Identification and transportability](../wiki/concepts/identification.md)

Here *inference* means reasoning from observations and assumptions to an uncertain target beyond what is directly observed. It covers estimation and uncertainty assessment, including methods that use no conventional significance cutoff. When the target is an unknown effect, translating an estimate into pounds or percentage points helps a reader understand its consequences. To establish the population magnitude you also need an argument for what the study has learned about that target. The next section shows how to make that argument for a specified statistical model.

## 2. What do the data tell you about the benchmark?

**Illustration.** Consider three hypothetical studies of a one-year reduction in default rates. Each identifies its target effect and gives an unbiased normal estimate with known standard error. All entries are in percentage points. The negligible bounds are fixed before looking at results and are assumed to be justified for each question. For each row, compare the interval with zero and with the stated bounds. Which study would you use to support practical negligibility?

| Study | Estimated reduction | Standard error | 95% confidence interval | Negligible region |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Small estimate, high precision | 0.10 | 0.02 | [0.0608, 0.1392] | (−1, 1) |
| Large estimate, low precision | 2.00 | 2.00 | [−1.9199, 5.9199] | (−1, 1) |
| Zero estimate, high precision | 0.00 | 0.001 | [−0.001960, 0.001960] | (−0.01, 0.01) |

The first interval excludes zero and sits well inside the negligible region. The compatible 90% interval also lies inside the bounds, so two one-sided tests at the 5% level support equivalence for that margin. You have evidence of a nonzero effect that is small enough to be negligible on the stated criterion. The second estimate looks large. Its interval includes reductions below and above one percentage point, large enough increases in defaults to matter, and effects near zero. A claim of a worthwhile reduction needs stronger evidence. The third study has a two-sided p-value of one against zero and a 90% interval that lies inside the much narrower negligible region. The evidence is informative about smallness despite the non-rejection. Each equivalence claim is conditional on its margin and model. [Reproduce the interval and equivalence calculations](../wiki/evidence/mathematical-checks.md)

Now return to the opening study and hold its estimate fixed. Increasing precision changes the likelihood for the unknown effect while the displayed estimate stays the same. An economist can agree that 1.1 percentage points would matter and still ask how precisely the study establishes that the effect clears one percentage point. Before relying on a narrow interval, ask what the design identifies and how model choice, multiplicity or selection affected the estimate and its calibration.

**Source claim.** Gelman and Carlin examine the risks of wrong signs and exaggerated magnitudes among estimates that survive a significance screen. Their design calculations depend on plausible effects specified independently of the focal estimate. [Gelman and Carlin, design analysis and discussion](../wiki/papers/gelman-carlin-2014.md)

**Source claim.** Morey et al. challenge the move from repeated-sampling coverage to a probability statement or a ranking of plausible values for a fixed parameter in a realised interval. The project consulted their abstract and introduction. **Project interpretation.** An interval’s coverage concerns the procedure under its assumptions. Where tests and intervals are mathematically compatible, the range can still help a reader compare the estimate with several meaningful benchmarks. If the reader only inspects whether it excludes zero, the original binary judgement returns. [Morey et al.](../wiki/papers/morey-et-al-2016-intervals.md) · [What can intervals solve?](../wiki/claims/C007-intervals.md)

There is a precise sense in which evidence for a worthwhile positive effect can entail significance against zero. Write β for the true reduction and δ for a positive benchmark. Suppose the criterion for establishing importance is a one-sided lower confidence bound L above δ. If L > δ > 0, then L > 0. Within the same compatible one-sided procedure and error level, rejecting H₀: β ≤ δ entails rejecting H₀: β ≤ 0. The single bound does both jobs under these conditions. Keep the tail and error level fixed when applying that implication. Whether to act remains a question about consequences under uncertainty. [Nested meaningful-effect tests](../wiki/claims/C011-nested-meaningful-tests.md)

## 3. Where do the competing arguments disagree?

The 2008 exchange is useful once you can tell which claim is at stake. Read the two sides with the opening example in view. Would you grant that the lender can define a meaningful reduction without a p-value? Would you also grant that the lender can judge whether the true reduction is above the benchmark without accounting for uncertainty?

**Source claim.** McCloskey and Ziliak make a case for substantive judgement. In their reply, p. 43 allows considered p-levels and power when sampling precision is relevant, and p. 52 rejects reducing all losses to money. Hoover and Siegler’s initial article distinguishes importance from precision on p. 2 and allows action on weak signals under asymmetric losses on p. 15. Their rejoinder, p. 59, asks whose losses ought to govern shared scientific knowledge. These concessions matter when deciding what either side is committed to. The three texts have complete readings recorded in the matched ledger. [McCloskey and Ziliak’s reply](../wiki/papers/mccloskey-ziliak-2008-reply.md) · [*Sound and fury*](../wiki/papers/hoover-siegler-2008-sound.md) · [Hoover and Siegler’s rejoinder](../wiki/papers/hoover-siegler-2008-rejoinder.md) · [Page-level evidence](../wiki/disagreements/2008-exchange.md)

**Project interpretation.** The strongest version of the contextual argument is that the data alone cannot determine what size of effect is worth attention or what a mistake would cost. The strongest inferential argument is that an economically literate interpretation of a noisy estimate can still be wrong about the size or sign of the population effect. A scientific report might make its uncertainty explicit precisely so that people with different objectives can use it. These arguments can both hold, and the exchange contains claims that go further.

Consider the reply’s separation of sampling precision from magnitude on p. 43. It holds for the printed value of an estimate. For an unknown effect, changing the standard error while holding the estimate fixed changes the likelihood for the effect in the normal model. The reply’s argument on pp. 49–50 about observing a single realised time series raises a question about the source of information. One realised path can be informative about a process under suitable assumptions about dependence and stability. Choosing a stochastic model still leaves those assumptions and any causal interpretation to be justified. [Reply and locators](../wiki/papers/mccloskey-ziliak-2008-reply.md) · [Identification and transportability](../wiki/concepts/identification.md)

Apply the same scrutiny to the inferential defence. On p. 25 of *Sound and fury*, non-rejection risks being read as imprecision and significance as a prerequisite for interpreting magnitude. The precise zero estimate in Section 2 shows where that reading breaks down. The technical claims can also be checked against the model:

- On p. 14, the stated limit for power near the null is zero. For the continuous normal test with size α held fixed, power tends to α as the alternative approaches the null. Power against nearby alternatives is still low.
- The claim on p. 17 also turns on how the statistic behaves. In a Gaussian regression with known error variance and a fixed true coefficient, let the residualised regressor’s sum of squares approach zero from above. The mean of the standardised statistic tends to zero and its variance stays one. The raw estimate becomes unstable while the test statistic remains random.
- The parenthesis on p. 58 of the rejoinder conflates a p-value with a Type I error probability. The p-value is a tail probability under the null and model. The Type I error probability describes a procedure’s rate of rejection under the null.

These are local corrections to arguments that still pose a serious challenge to relying on estimated magnitude alone. The ASA’s p-value and importance distinction provides another check, with the project’s reading limits recorded in the note. [*Sound and fury*](../wiki/papers/hoover-siegler-2008-sound.md) · [Rejoinder](../wiki/papers/hoover-siegler-2008-rejoinder.md) · [Derivations](../wiki/evidence/mathematical-checks.md) · [ASA statement and reading record](../wiki/papers/wasserstein-lazar-2016.md)

**Open question.** What would count as evidence that a particular statistical practice distorts applied conclusions? The logical limits of a zero-null test and the prevalence of its misuse are separate questions. Hoover and Siegler state on p. 65 of their rejoinder that the book was unavailable when they wrote their article and rejoinder. The appendix examines selected book passages and the original audit materials directly, with limits on what can be inferred about published practice. ## 4. What remains uncertain when a standard error gets smaller?

Imagine two analysts give the lender very precise estimates from the same data. They made different defensible choices about how to define the target population or adjust for other factors. Before comparing the standard errors, check that they estimated the same effect. A standard error describes sampling variability for a specified analysis. A second analysis can reveal that the substantively relevant conclusion depends on what was modelled, which assumptions identify the target or how results were selected. The targeted 2024–2026 update added three contributions on these questions and brought the catalogue to 35 works. It records its coverage and reading limits, including for candidates outside the core. [Search and selection record](../sources/update-2024-2026.md) · [Source catalogue and reading depths](../sources/literature-map.md)

### Comparing analyses of the same data

**Source claim.** Menkveld et al. (2024) study variation across analysts using common data and report that peer feedback reduces dispersion. The chapter draws on the abstract, introduction and selected sections including II.D–III. **Project interpretation.** First check whether analysts answer the same question. Then ask how the results change across defensible analysis paths and which parts of that variation your decision depends on. Using dispersion to estimate bias or as an independent variance term would require further assumptions. Publication selection can affect both bias and dispersion. [Menkveld et al.](../wiki/papers/menkveld-etal-2024-nonstandard-errors.md) · [Uncertainty beyond sampling](../wiki/concepts/uncertainty-beyond-sampling.md)

**Illustration.** A prespecified hypothetical portfolio has estimated gross alpha of 20 basis points per month with a known standard error of 2 basis points. Here alpha is the intercept in the specified factor model, measured before trading costs. Assume an unbiased normal estimate under the stipulated model and trading regime. At a known, fixed cost of 10 basis points per month the estimated net alpha is 10. Raise the cost to 25 and the net estimate is −5, with a 95% interval of −8.92 to −1.08 basis points per month. The estimate of gross alpha and its standard error stay fixed. The cost shifts the target and the conclusion about net alpha. If you compare two studies of this trade, ask which costs, factors and levels of trading their results represent. [Gross-to-net exercise](../exercises/05-finance-identification.md) · [Finance and replication](../wiki/concepts/finance.md)

### Setting a margin worth testing

**Source claim.** Fitzgerald’s 2025 working paper reanalyses published null claims with equivalence tests. Page 22 distinguishes failure to establish equivalence from evidence of a large relationship. Page 34 distinguishes generic margins for a literature audit from margins suited to individual questions. The project consulted selected sections. **Project interpretation.** This is a way to check how much evidence the papers provide for negligibility under stated margins. Going from failed equivalence to a claim about the frequency of false nulls or unacceptable Type II errors requires more information about the true effects and the procedure. [Fitzgerald](../wiki/papers/fitzgerald-2025-equivalence.md) · [Critical equivalence audit](../wiki/disagreements/equivalence-audit.md)

**Illustration.** Suppose every true effect is zero, each unbiased normal estimate has a known standard error of one percentage point, and the fixed negligible region is ±0.1 percentage points. The 90% interval’s half-width is about 1.645 percentage points. It cannot fit inside the region anywhere, so every equivalence test fails under the compatible 5% procedure even though all the effects are exactly zero. Before interpreting a failure rate, work out what the procedure could have established at plausible precisions. A wide fixed margin can make equivalence easier to establish while preserving calibration for the changed null. The justification for that substantive claim is separate from the error-control problem introduced when analysts adapt margins to results. [Equivalence calculations](../wiki/evidence/mathematical-checks.md) · [Margins and alternatives](../wiki/concepts/equivalence.md)

For the lender, deciding whether a programme breaks even also changes which threshold matters. Write V for the positive value in pounds per borrower of a one-percentage-point reduction, and c for the cost in pounds per borrower over the same horizon. If values and costs are fixed and benefits are linear, an effect β below c/V gives negative net benefit. A symmetric equivalence rule requiring |β| < c/V would also have to rule out large harmful effects. Those effects already make adoption unattractive. Choose a hypothesis that matches the economic question, and bring uncertainty in V or c into the analysis if the decision depends on it.

### Learning about an effect that is only partly identified

**Source claim.** In the framework examined by Aradillas Fernández et al. (2026), criteria evaluated before and after observing data can recommend different robust Bayesian rules, and randomisation can be optimal under either. The selected material includes the abstract and conclusion. The results rely on a specified normal signal, linear mappings, a convex symmetric parameter set and a restrictive prior class. The authors leave the comparative merits of the criteria open. **Project interpretation.** A larger sample can pin down what the signal measures while leaving a range of payoff-relevant effects consistent with it. What to do then depends on which states, priors and losses the decision criterion admits. [Aradillas Fernández et al. and scope of reading](../wiki/papers/aradillas-fernandez-etal-2026-robust-bayes.md)

## 5. What can justify an action under uncertainty?

**Illustration.** Consider a programme whose unknown average gross benefit is measured in pounds per eligible person over one year. The estimated benefit is £400 and its known standard error is £500. Assume valid identification and an unbiased normal estimate. The two-sided p-value for zero benefit is approximately 0.4237. The policy choice is whether to adopt at a known cost or keep the status quo at zero payoff. Suppose the decision-maker uses a proper normal prior on gross benefit, with mean £0 and standard deviation £1,000, and values net benefits linearly. Before calculating, ask whether you would expect the same recommendation at a cost of £100 and at £500.

Combining the likelihood with the prior gives a posterior mean of £320 and standard deviation of £447.21. Under linear, risk-neutral utility, subtract the known cost from the posterior mean. At £100 the expected net benefit is £220 and the criterion favours adoption. At £500 it is −£180 and favours the status quo. The study, prior and p-value are identical in the two cases. The change is in the consequences of acting. The sample leaves the true gross benefit uncertain, and the recommendation depends on the prior, cost and choice of utility. [Checked normal-prior calculation](../wiki/evidence/mathematical-checks.md) · [Decision memo exercise](../exercises/06-decision-memo.md)

**Project interpretation.** This gives a counterexample to a universal requirement to reject zero before acting. The calculation fixes the action set and the cost, and sets learning, delay, irreversibility and distributional objectives aside. A different prior or a penalty for losses below break-even can change the choice. In an application, defend the assumptions to the person who bears the consequences. [Manski (2019), treatment choice](../wiki/papers/manski-2019-treatment-choice.md) · [Kandel and Stambaugh, investment application at abstract depth](../wiki/papers/kandel-stambaugh-1996-asset-allocation.md)

A choice given the observed data is one task. Designing a rule before the data arrive is another. To evaluate a rule at a possible state, imagine sampling repeatedly in that state and applying the rule each time. Expected regret is the gap between the best welfare attainable if you knew the state and the rule’s average welfare over those samples. A minimax-regret approach chooses the rule that makes the largest such gap across feasible states as small as possible. Another possibility is that one action does better in every relevant state, so the ordering is robust to how you weight those states. The strength of that conclusion depends on how you justify the set of states, the sampling model and the measure of welfare. [Decisions under uncertainty](../wiki/concepts/decisions.md)

**Source claim.** The verified final abstract of Armstrong, Kitagawa and Tetenov (2026) places hypothesis testing, estimation and preanalysis plans within Wald’s decision framework. The detailed final paper remains a priority for reading. **Project interpretation.** A test can be evaluated as a decision rule. The question is whether its hypotheses and error costs fit the problem, in comparison with other rules that can be applied to the same data. [Final abstract and version record](../sources/update-2024-2026.md)

Return to Hoover and Siegler’s question about whose losses govern scientific knowledge. A study can report an estimate, its uncertainty and the design assumptions in a form that different users can examine. An investor, a bank and a regulator may agree on what the study establishes and make different choices because they face different constraints or value the consequences differently. A common evidential standard can coexist with different actions. [2008 exchange on loss and scientific evidence](../wiki/disagreements/2008-exchange.md) · [Compare rules in the decision lab](../simulations/labs/03-decisions.md)

## 6. What would change the conclusion?

**Project judgement.** Defining economic importance can precede significance testing. Establishing the magnitude of an unknown population or causal effect generally requires a defensible account of identification and uncertainty. Whether that account entails rejecting zero depends on the evidential claim and procedure. Action can be justified without such rejection when the consequences and decision criterion warrant it under uncertainty. This is a conditional judgement about method. It leaves room to revise both the evidential claim and the decision as the assumptions or data change.

**Open questions.**

- **Research practice.** Complete the article crosswalk, recover the expanded coding data and test passage-level classifications with independent readers before judging prevalence. Reconcile the original 1980s coding record where possible and check weights and reliability. Direct reading of the final Darby and Bernheim–Wantz texts settles particular descriptions. Identification and replication remain open. The remaining book chapters and endnotes, and the primary evidence for historical, medical and broader welfare-harm claims, are still required for a wider verdict. [Book reading record](../wiki/evidence/cult-2008-reading.md) · [Final-source review](../reviews/2026-10-24-final-source-review.md)
- **Equivalence.** Replicate the estimates, examine exclusions and dependence among observations, and vary economically defensible margins. Estimating the proportion of claimed negligible effects that actually lie outside justified margins requires evidence beyond the failure rate. That proportion also answers a different question from the probability of a Type II error in a zero-null test at a specified alternative. [Equivalence audit](../wiki/disagreements/equivalence-audit.md)
- **Finance.** Compare estimands before attributing dispersion to analysis choices. Ask how costs, capacity and risk connect gross alpha to the payoff and utility relevant for a particular investor. Evidence on the source of analyst and replication differences could change which uncertainties deserve the most weight. [Finance and replication](../wiki/concepts/finance.md)
- **Decision rules.** Check proofs and applications, compare wider prior classes and read the final Armstrong, Kitagawa and Tetenov paper. A different model of what is identified, whose losses count or which actions are feasible can change the assessment of the rules. [Robust Bayes paper note](../wiki/papers/aradillas-fernandez-etal-2026-robust-bayes.md) · [Priority reading](../sources/update-2024-2026.md)

For your own project, write three sentences using the same empirical result. Say what size of effect would matter and to whom. Say what the study establishes about that magnitude under its assumptions. Then say which action you would recommend under a stated criterion, what additional information it required and what would change your mind. Ask a colleague to challenge one premise in each sentence. [Magnitude, evidence and action exercise](../exercises/01-magnitude-evidence-action.md) · [Decision memo and worked solution](../exercises/06-decision-memo.md)

## Appendix. Auditing claims about research practice

The 2008 debate includes claims about how economists actually work. Suppose you want to check a statement that a large share of articles confuses statistical and economic significance. First identify the articles and the scoring rule. Then ask whether a reader applying that rule to the original text would reach the same judgement, and whether that judgement answers the question you care about. The audit examines these steps for particular versions and cases.

To establish the size of an unknown effect, that programme still needs methods that link the observations to the target. The direct reading covers printed pp. 57–61 and 74–85 in an authorised preview. The published pages, prepublication preface and user-supplied 2012 lecture have separate evidence records. ### What does a positive or adverse score mean?

**Source claim.** Book Table 6.1 reports that 80.2% discuss coefficient sizes and that 29.7% consider more than significance decisive. A paper could do the first and still fail the second criterion. ### What changes when you read the original papers?

**Project interpretation.** For each case, ask whether the critics describe the article accurately, whether the method is warranted and whether the economic conclusion is true. The records below support bounded judgements about the first two questions. The underlying estimates and economic mechanisms would require additional work.

**Darby (1984).** The published article was fully read, with disputed pages independently checked. Page 311 translates a coefficient into approximately −0.46 and +0.61 percentage-point adjustments to annual log growth and retains a control with t = 0.31. Page 315 gives an economic explanation of a statistically non-significant coefficient difference. Those passages support Hoover and Siegler’s narrow defence on magnitude. The oil-price exclusion and uncertainty after model selection remain open. Their explanation by confidence-interval containment leaves a covariance-aware equality test to be established separately. [Final Darby reading and disputed pages](../wiki/evidence/darby-final-followup.md)

**Woodbury and Spiegelman (1987).** The selected published text and Tables 7–8 discuss costs, displacement and a scenario with full take-up and unchanged benefit reductions that reverses the apparent fiscal attraction. This helps explain caution about scaling the programme. A benefit–cost ratio should be compared with one to assess fiscal break-even, and a policy decision also needs an account of participation and possible spillovers. Concluding that only one subgroup responded needs evidence about the contrast and its uncertainty. The countercritique’s comparisons of within-group significance leave that issue open. Table 6 and the detailed report await reading and may contain additional tests. [Trial, subgroup and benefit–cost audit](../wiki/evidence/aer-darby-woodbury.md)

**Bernanke and Blinder (1992).** In the published tables and empirical discussion examined, the tables are unranked and unstarred, and the prose uses significance to rank predictive performance. Variance decompositions add a substantive comparison, with qualifications about variable ordering and identification. Check both the table and the interpretation before deciding whether the disputed description fits. [Monetary transmission case](../wiki/evidence/aer-macro-finance-addiction.md)

**Bernheim and Wantz (1995).** Selected empirical and concluding sections in the final paper were read directly. Pages 543–544 distinguish and interpret economic effects. Table 4’s star marks an alternative test, panel A gives magnitudes and standard errors, and n. 17 reports direct tests of differences. This confirms specific corrections from Hoover and Siegler and adds evidence beyond the draft table. It changes the criticism that the paper only compared separate significance outcomes. The remaining questions include stability inferred from non-rejection and identification of tax effects. [Final Bernheim–Wantz reading and version comparison](../wiki/evidence/bernheim-wantz-final-followup.md)

**Becker, Grossman and Murphy (1994).** The published empirical discussion examines elasticities and implausible discount rates and reports sensitivity analysis. Pages 408–410 show the authors resisting a mechanical response to an adverse Hausman result. That is evidence about how they used the test in choosing a specification. Instrument validity, aggregation, possible input screening and a local interpretation of non-rejection as validity remain open concerns. [Addiction case and locators](../wiki/evidence/aer-macro-finance-addiction.md)

The direct readings strengthen the corrections to blanket accounts of magnitude neglect in Darby and Bernheim–Wantz, while leaving specific questions about filtering, non-rejection and subgroup interpretation. They also identify where defences in the countercritique stop short of justifying the economic inference. The five articles were selected in an adversarial exchange, so the reach of the audit is the particular claims it checks. A prevalence estimate would require an explicit article universe and a defensible measurement design. To practise, choose one disputed claim and identify the estimand, the supporting passage, the statistical step at issue and the evidence you would need to go further.
